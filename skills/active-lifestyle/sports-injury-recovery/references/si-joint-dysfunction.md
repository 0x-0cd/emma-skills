# SI Joint Dysfunction Reference

## Anatomy

The **sacroiliac (SI) joint** connects the sacrum (base of spine) to the left and right ilia (pelvic bones). It is a stiff synovial joint with <4° of motion. Its primary roles:

- **Shock absorption** — transmits forces between upper body and lower limbs during walking/running
- **Force closure** — muscles (glutes, core, pelvic floor) compress the joint to create dynamic stability
- **Form closure** — bony interlocking provides passive stability

## Pain Pattern

| Feature | Typical Presentation |
|---|---|
| **Location** | Unilateral, 1–2 fingers above the gluteus maximus, around the PSIS dimple |
| **Quality** | Dull ache, sharp on provocation, sometimes burning |
| **Aggravators** | Single-leg stance, running, pushing off, stairs, rolling over in bed |
| **Relievers** | SI belt, side-lying on affected side, walking on flat ground (mild cases) |
| **Radiation** | Often stays local; can refer to groin (anterior) or down posterior thigh (but rarely below knee) |

## Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | How to Distinguish |
|---|---|
| **Coccydynia (tailbone pain)** | Central pain; sitting on hard surface hurts; pain at very bottom of spine |
| **Proximal hamstring tendinopathy** | Pain is lower — at the ischial tuberosity ("sit bone") — not "above the glute"; hamstring-dominant movements trigger it |
| **Lumbar disc / facet** | Bending forward / rotating triggers it; may radiate below knee |
| **Piriformis syndrome** | Pain deeper in buttock; positive figure-4 stretch; may have sciatica-like symptoms |
| **Gluteus medius tendinopathy** | Pain more lateral (side of hip); positive single-leg stance but tender spot is on greater trochanter, not SI dimple |

## Common Causes in Basketball Players

1. **Asymmetrical loading** — right-handed players tend to jump off the left foot for layups, overloading the left SI joint
2. **Glute medius weakness** — the #1 root cause. Weak hip abductors fail to stabilize the pelvis during single-leg stance → SI joint takes the shear
3. **Poor landing mechanics** — landing stiff-legged or twisted after rebounds
4. **SI belt as temporary solution** — external compression helps during the acute phase but doesn't fix the underlying muscle imbalance

## Rehab Progression

### Phase 1: Acute (Pain on high-impact)
- **DO**: Pelvic tilts, single knee-to-chest, double-leg glute bridges, clamshells
- **DON'T**: Run, jump, deep lunges, heavy single-leg work
- **SI belt**: Wear during walking if it helps

### Phase 2: Sub-acute (Walking pain-free, running still triggers)
- **DO**: Single-leg glute bridge, bird-dog, side planks (from knees), lateral band walks
- **Progress**: Add resistance bands, increase reps/sets based on 24-hour symptom response

### Phase 3: Return to Sport
- **DO**: Slow jog → sport drills without contact → controlled scrimmage
- **Key test**: Single-leg hop for distance (pain-free = ready)

## Key Clinical Pearls

- SI joint dysfunction accounts for **15–30%** of mechanical low back pain
- The interosseous sacroiliac ligament is the **strongest ligament in the human body**
- Women have **~2.3x more SI joint mobility** than men (hormonal + anatomical differences)
- Cluster testing is more useful to **rule out** SI joint pain than to confirm it (92% specificity, 35% sensitivity)
- "Force closure" (dynamic compression from muscle) is the primary stability mechanism during movement — if muscles are weak or inhibited, the joint becomes unstable
